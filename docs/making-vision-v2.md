## Abstract

Vision is often described as though its primary task were to turn photons into recognized objects.

That is too narrow.

An organism does not encounter the world as an empty visual field waiting to be labelled. It already possesses persistent knowledge, spatial maps, object and material knowledge, expectations, learned behaviours, inherited information, social information, current needs, physiological state and action machinery.

Vision enters that already-functioning system and supplies current information to it.

But vision is not merely an API into a database either.

A genuinely unfamiliar environment can still be navigated. A novel surface can still be matched. Moving neighbours can still be tracked. Free space can still be extracted. Apparent depth can alter action. Partial objects can generate useful candidates. A degraded visual representation can remain sufficient for some actions while becoming insufficient for reading or fine discrimination.

Current visual structure can therefore be useful before the organism knows what the thing is.

At the same time, the organism cannot deeply process everything it receives. Processing has costs. What becomes worth additional processing depends on current sensory information, persistent knowledge, expectations, physiological and motivational state, available energy, current needs, uncertainty, risk, learned relevance and the statistical structure of the environment.

Making Vision V2 therefore treats biological vision as a selective allocation system operating on current sensory structure and continuously recalibrating that allocation to the organism's current sensory field and internal state.

The central problem is not:
- How does vision recognize everything?
- It is:

How does an organism allocate limited processing across incomplete, changing, internally and externally generated information while continuously adapting that allocation to the environment?

V2 retains the central V1 insight:

Current visual computation + persistent knowledge + selective promotion + state-dependent allocation.

It expands it by separating recognition from representation, candidate generation from object identity, promotion from generic attention, learning from storage, current sensory information from persistent spatial knowledge, apparent geometry from physical geometry, and external acquisition from internal projection.

It also adds:

dimensional impoverishment; degraded visual information and task dissociations; temporal construction;

cue compression and substitution;

apparent-depth/action calibration; environmental stimulus-regime adaptation; recalibration after environmental change;

behavioural commitment as a readout;

map and action-rule staleness; energy and physiological state as allocation variables; stronger treatment of cross-modal correction;

more explicit research tests.

- smell

An object therefore has:

- It has characteristic ways of:
- extending, flying, landing, turning, fleeing.
- A predator does not merely know prey shape.
- It may know:

A predator following prey behind vegetation does not necessarily need to invent a new trajectory model from scratch. Existing knowledge about what that prey tends to do can contribute to what it expects next.

Affordance theory provides a strong independent correspondence here. Gibson's ecological approach explicitly treats objects in terms of what they afford an organism, rather than requiring useful perception to terminate first in an abstract object label.

Modern object-action research likewise treats object knowledge as containing action and manipulation information rather than only visual identity.

## 3. Object knowledge is individual

Two members of the same species do not necessarily possess the same object knowledge.

The Punch/wiper example remains useful.

For one monkey:
- wiper → familiar material/object → manipulable → acceptable.
- For another:
- wiper → unfamiliar strange thing → uncertainty → caution.
- The physical object is identical.
- The persistent knowledge attached to it is not.
- Thus:
- same species + different developmental experience → different persistent object

knowledge → different future promotion and behaviour.

Object familiarity is therefore not merely an objective property of the object.

It is partly a property of the relationship between object and organism.

This is why “the object is familiar” is incomplete.

The more useful statement is:
- the organism has a sufficiently cheap and useful representation of this object.

## 4. Spatial knowledge is not a photograph

An organism can maintain persistent spatial knowledge without maintaining a detailed visual recording of every moment.

A spatial representation can contain:
- locations
- layout
- routes
- landmarks
- stable relationships
- dangerous places
- recurring destinations.
- Persistent spatial knowledge tells the organism:
- where things generally are.
- Current sensory information tells it:
- what is there now.
- These are different.
- A chair being moved does not necessarily require rewriting the entire room representation.

A new painting may eventually become part of familiar spatial knowledge.

A tiny temporary object may never become part of it.

A dangerous change may be incorporated much more rapidly.

Cognitive-map research independently supports map-like relational spatial representations and flexible navigation rather than requiring a photographic record of the environment.

## 5. Local spatial structure

For immediate action, the organism does not need its entire spatial knowledge simultaneously.

A relevant local portion can become operationally available.

V1 called this a:
- maplet / local inset.

The underlying concept remains useful, but V2 treats maplet as Making Vision terminology rather than an established neuroscientific unit.

The local spatial structure combines:
- persistent spatial relationships
- current sensory information
- current body position
- immediate action requirements.
- It can support:

where to step where to reach where the door is where the obstacle is where the next route segment lies.

The persistent spatial representation supplies a skeleton.

Current sensory acquisition supplies what has changed.

## 6. The current sensory field

V2 retires the V1 term board.

This is deliberate.

“Board” has a different architectural meaning in the Memory Prism framework and cannot safely carry both meanings once Making Vision also incorporates energy, physiological state, hormonal state and resource allocation.

V2 therefore uses:
- current sensory field
- to mean:

the currently accessible external situation as available to the organism through its sensory systems.

The current sensory field is not the physical universe.

It is not persistent memory.

It is not an internal world model.

It is not the organism's energetic state.

It is the currently accessible external situation from which sensory systems can acquire information.

A bee and a human can occupy the same physical location while having different accessible sensory fields because their sensory systems admit different information.

The current sensory field can contain:
- surfaces
- objects
- people
- pathways
- movement
- light
- shadows
- openings
- obstacles
- temporary opportunities
- temporal changes.

Its accessible information is constrained by the organism's sensory apparatus before higher-level allocation and interpretation occur.

This retains the V1 species-relative point while removing the terminology collision.

## 8. Vision has direct access to current structure

Vision is not merely:
- current photons → database lookup.

- The person does not need to know what every object is.

Therefore vision has at least two complementary sources of useful information:
- Current sensory computation

- Persistent knowledge

- Persistent knowledge can dramatically amplify current perception.
- It is not a prerequisite for useful current sensory computation.

## 10. Structural extraction is not necessarily serial

V1 sometimes allowed an overly neat picture:
- structure → candidate → object → action.
- V2 does not require that sequence.
- Current structure and prior knowledge can interact.
- Object and scene processing can interact.

Scene context can influence object interpretation, while objects can influence scene interpretation. Contemporary reviews describe object and scene processing as partly parallel and bidirectionally interacting rather than as a simple serial pipeline.

Therefore V2 uses:
- interacting operations
- rather than:
- one mandatory processing sequence.

## 11. Candidates are hypotheses, not objects

- Partial contours, components, movement, context and relations can generate candidates.

A candidate is a processing hypothesis.

It may be:
- strong
- weak
- ambiguous
- familiar-looking
- novel
- partially visible
- behaviourally relevant
- ultimately rejected.
- Candidate generation can occur before stable object recognition.
- Candidate rejection is itself a mechanism.
- The system can generate:
- “something may be there”
- without committing to:
- “I know what that thing is.”

The V2 inventory explicitly separates candidate generation, candidate confidence, candidate selection and candidate rejection.

## 12. Recognition is not necessarily image retrieval

A familiar encounter can become cheap because existing representation provides sufficient correspondence.

But the representation need not be a photograph.

It can contain:
- diagnostic shape
- proportions
- components
- material
- behaviour
- context
- relations
- affordances
- consequences.
- A strong match can permit cheap recognition.
- A partial match can trigger recognition plus verification.
- A poor match can trigger promotion and deeper inspection.
- No useful match can trigger construction of a new representation.
- Therefore:
- recognition can use stored structure without being retrieval of a stored picture.

Recognition and identification remain separate.

An organism can recognise:
- “familiar”
- without being able to:
- name, classify or fully identify.

## 13. Dimensional impoverishment

V2 adds a direct question:
- How much dimensional information does perception actually require?

Consider:
- real 3D object → photograph → silhouette → cutout.
- A rich object supplies:
- width
- height
- depth
- volume
- physical occlusion
- curvature
- texture
- material cues.
- A planar representation can preserve:
- contour
- width

- It loses or alters:

The important question is not simply:

## 14. Planar information may be operationally sufficient

Dimensional reduction does not imply total information loss.

Contour can survive.

Proportion can survive.

Component relationships can survive.

Diagnostic markings can survive.

Some affordances can remain inferable.

Therefore:
- loss of full dimensional information does not imply loss of all useful object information.

A system may operate directly on planar information without constructing a complete three-dimensional object.

Whether it does so for a particular task is an empirical question.

## 21. Lazy processing

The organism cannot deeply process everything.

## 24. Perturbation

A perturbation is information that escapes cheap background treatment and becomes potentially worth further processing.

It can be:
- unexpected movement
- unexpected location
- missing expected object
- unusual object

- relevant sensory event

## 25. Promotion

The critical transition remains:
- background → candidate → promoted candidate/event

- Promotion is therefore an allocation operation.

## 30. Energy is part of the allocation problem

Processing costs resources.

Exploration costs resources.

Movement costs resources.

Memory maintenance costs resources.

Risk can cost survival.

Therefore an allocation architecture cannot treat all potentially useful information as equally worth processing.

The relevant question is:
- What is worth spending resources on now?
- This does not mean that every visual computation is consciously or explicitly budgeted.

It means that biological processing operates under energetic and physiological constraints.

V2 therefore treats energy as a constraint on the allocation system rather than as a property of the current sensory field itself.

## 31. Hormonal and physiological state can alter promotion

V2 adds a specific route by which internal state can influence visual allocation:
- physiological state → altered valuation/sensitivity → altered promotion.

There is empirical evidence for state-dependent modulation of visual processing.

For example, hunger has been shown to alter attentional capture by food-related visual stimuli, with hungry participants showing greater capture in one established paradigm.

More recent work also finds that hunger/satiety can alter visual attention to food cues, although the direction and conditions of the effect are not uniform across paradigms.

This is important precisely because V2 should not turn “hormones promote objects” into a universal rule.

The defensible claim is:
- physiological state can alter the allocation value of sensory information.

The specific hormone, pathway and task must be established separately.

## 47. The wet-floor example

A wet floor provides a minimal case.

The immediate problem is not:
- dry floor.
- It is:
- don't step there while it is slippery.
- A conspicuous wet patch can function as a temporary landmark:
- environmental change → visible perturbation → promotion → altered foot placement.

If evaporation can solve the underlying problem, the person may not need to spend additional effort mopping immediately.

The intervention can instead be:
- change the sensory field enough to prevent the dangerous action.
- This illustrates a broader principle:

organisms do not have to solve every environmental problem directly if they can cheaply alter their interaction with it.

## 48. Camouflage: anti-promotion

Camouflage provides an unusually useful counterexample to promotion.

The camouflaged organism is not necessarily trying to make the observer identify it as another species.

Often the desired outcome is simpler:
- remain non-actionable.
- The organism attempts to remain within:
- background
- surface
- environmental texture
- expected structure.

In functional terms:
- don't become the candidate worth additional processing.
- Camouflage therefore provides a useful hypothesis about the other side of the promotion boundary.

But V2 must not overstate it.

Camouflage strategies differ.

Background matching, disruptive coloration, masquerade and motion camouflage do not necessarily defeat exactly the same stage of an observer's processing.

A large meta-analysis found that camouflage increased predator search time overall and reduced attack rate, with different strategies producing different effects.

Thus the defensible MV claim is:

some camouflage strategies reduce the probability or reliability of becoming an actionable perceptual candidate.

## 49. Camouflage is conditional

Camouflage-capable organisms do not necessarily maintain maximal camouflage continuously.

Deployment depends on:
- exposure
- predator presence
- environmental background
- lighting
- movement
- current state
- cost.
- The capability can exist continuously while expensive deployment remains conditional.

This is structurally similar to promotion itself:
- capability available ≠ capability continuously deployed.

## 54. Map staleness

- Later the original arrangement can return.
- The organism may retain both action patterns and select according to current context.

## 57. Sleep is not sensory death

Sleep is not equivalent to total information-processing shutdown.

Sensory information can still be processed without producing awakening.

Sleep also participates in memory consolidation and reactivation. Research has documented replay of waking neural activity during sleep and continuing investigation of how sensory processing and memory reactivation interact.

Therefore:
- offline from ordinary interaction ≠ offline from information processing.

V2 should avoid the stronger V1 phrase “deep repair” as a general explanation.

The useful claim is narrower:

sleep changes the operating state while permitting substantial internal processing and memory-related activity.

## 62. Learning loop

The system is recursive.

Existing knowledge guides current interpretation.

Current encounters generate new structure.

New structure can be retained.

Retained structure changes:
- future candidate generation
- future recognition cost
- future promotion
- cue diagnosticity
- action shortcuts.
- Therefore:
- learning changes what the organism will notice next.
- This is one of the most important consequences of Making Vision.
- The system is not merely processing the environment.
- It is continually changing the processing system that will encounter the environment next.

## 79. The central loop

The core loop is not:
- see → identify → remember.
- It is closer to:

current sensory input → economical processing → candidate/change → promotion when warranted → deeper processing → action and/or persistence

while:
- persistent knowledge + internal state + environmental statistics + current consequences
- continuously influence what is worth processing.
- At the same time:

current sensory input → useful structure → immediate action can operate without requiring object identification or database retrieval first.

## 80. Familiar sensory fields

A familiar environment can feel visually rich without requiring detailed processing of everything.

Persistent spatial knowledge supplies:
- structure.
- Local spatial knowledge supplies:
- relevant organization.
- Current vision supplies:
- present detail.
- Expectations reduce the need to deeply inspect unchanged information.

Then:
- object moved object missing unexpected movement unexpected obstacle familiar cue failed
- can produce perturbation and possible promotion.
- The efficient compromise is:
- remember the structure, sample the present, investigate the exceptions.

## 82. Novelty, risk and information value

- Novelty is not danger.
- Danger is not novelty.
- Information value is not novelty.

- Another can be:
- novel + low consequence + ignorable.
- The allocation system therefore needs more than a novelty detector.

It needs some interaction among:
- current state + knowledge + current sensory structure + anticipated consequence + processing cost.

The exact biological implementation remains an empirical question.

## 83. Persistence is an energetic architectural choice

Persistent knowledge is not free.

Durable information can require:
- storage
- connectivity
- retrieval
- maintenance
- updating
- interference management.
- Therefore persistence should be selective.
- Location and landmark structure may persist.
- Transient decorative detail may not.
- A dramatic event may persist after one exposure.
- A trivial object may disappear after repeated exposure.
- Persistence is therefore not simply:

p y

number of times seen.

It is closer to:
- what the organism has reason and capacity to retain.

## 84. Vision and persistent knowledge are reciprocal

- Persistent knowledge helps interpret current sensory information.
- Current sensory encounters update persistent knowledge.
- The loop is:
- existing knowledge → interpretation/promotion
- current encounter → learning
- learning → modified future processing.
- The system therefore changes itself through experience.
- An animal that once investigated an object may later treat it as ordinary.

A child who learns a tool's affordances no longer needs to discover them from scratch.

A predator that learns a prey pattern can detect it more efficiently.

The organism is continually changing the machinery that determines what it will process next.

The organism already has:

## 91. The simplest statement of Making Vision V2

A biological visual system does not need to deeply understand everything it receives.

It needs to:
- 1. Acquire current sensory structure from the current sensory field.

2. Operate under incomplete and noisy sensory access.

3. Use existing knowledge where available.

4. Extract useful structure without requiring stable object identification.

5. Generate candidates from partial, spatial and temporal evidence.

6. Maintain expectations about recurring environmental structure.

7. Allocate processing unevenly.

8. Promote some candidates, states, regions or events for additional processing.

9. Use internal state, energy, physiological condition, needs and consequences to alter allocation.

10. Use direct sensory structure for action even without full classification.

11. Use cross-modal and motor information to test current representations.

12. Retain useful information selectively.

13. Allow learning to change future recognition, cue use and promotion thresholds.

14. Adapt processing economy to the statistical regime of the environment.

15. Recalibrate after changes in that regime.

16. Allow the environment itself to become part of the information-management loop.

17. Continue operating without requiring complete reconstruction of the world.

The result is neither a camera nor a database.

It is a:

Making Vision does not attempt to explain all cognition.

It does not attempt to fully explain:
- consciousness
- all dreams

- all decision theory
- pathology
- every sensory illusion
- every evolutionary adaptation
- social learning as a complete field
- distributed intelligence as a complete field
- all forms of memory
- every hormonal mechanism
- every attentional mechanism.
- Those systems intersect with vision.
- They are not all the problem.
- The V2 question is narrower:

How does an organism allocate limited processing across incomplete, changing, internally and externally generated sensory information when it already possesses persistent knowledge, spatial structure, expectations, internal state and action machinery, and when its processing economy itself changes with experience and environmental regime?

The current answer is:

By combining direct current sensory computation with persistent knowledge and spatial structure, generating and selectively promoting candidates, allocating processing according to expectation, perturbation, need, state, energy, consequence and learned relevance, using action and cross-modal information to test representations, and continuously recalibrating that allocation to the statistical structure of the organism's environment.
